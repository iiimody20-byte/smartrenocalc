Full House Renovation Cost in 2026: Complete Budget Guide by Size, Scope & ROI

By the SmartRenoCalc Team · Last updated September 2026

Quick Answer: A full house renovation in 2026 typically costs $15 to $200+ per square foot, depending on scope. A cosmetic refresh (paint, flooring, fixtures, no structural or systems work) runs $15–$40/sq ft. A mid-range renovation touching the kitchen, one or two bathrooms, and general updates runs $50–$100/sq ft. A full gut renovation, stripped to the studs with new electrical, plumbing, and HVAC, runs $100–$200+/sq ft. On a typical 2,000-square-foot home, that's roughly $30,000 at the low end to $400,000+ for a complete gut. Labor accounts for 40–50% of most budgets.

Key takeaways:

  • National data puts a whole-house renovation on a 1,250–1,600 sq. ft. home at roughly $19,500–$88,400 for standard scopes — but a full gut on the same home can run well past $150,000.
  • The three "invisible" systems (electrical, plumbing, and HVAC) commonly total $20,000–$70,000 combined on a full gut renovation, before a single visible finish is chosen.
  • Full house renovations carry the lowest average ROI of any renovation category SmartRenoCalc tracks, roughly 45–65% depending on tier, because a large share of the budget replaces things buyers already expect to work.
  • Costs vary significantly by ZIP code and by how much of the project is "reward" spending (kitchen, baths, finishes) versus "expected condition" spending (systems, structure).

A full house renovation is the biggest budgeting decision most homeowners will make, and the hardest one to price from a single number. "Renovating the whole house" can mean a weekend of paint and new light fixtures, or it can mean stripping every wall down to the studs and rebuilding the plumbing, wiring, and HVAC from scratch — and those two projects can differ in cost by a factor of ten on the exact same square footage.

This guide breaks down 2026 pricing by scope and home size, shows where the money actually goes on a typical full house project, and walks through the financing and ROI questions that matter more here than on any single-room remodel.

What Determines Full House Renovation Cost in 2026

Every full-house estimate is built from the same handful of variables. Understanding them is the fastest way to sanity-check a contractor's number before you commit to anything:

  • Scope, above everything else. Whether the project is a cosmetic refresh, a mid-range update, or a full gut is the single biggest lever on price — bigger than home size, region, or finish choice combined.
  • Home size. Total square footage renovated scales the project linearly once scope is fixed, though fixed costs like permits and mobilization don't shrink with a smaller home.
  • Structural changes. Removing a load-bearing wall, changing the footprint, or reworking the layout adds engineering, framing, and permitting costs on top of everything else.
  • Age and condition of the home. Older homes hide more behind the walls, from knob-and-tube wiring to galvanized pipe and undersized framing, and almost none of it is visible until demolition starts.
  • Which systems get touched. A renovation that leaves the electrical, plumbing, and HVAC alone costs a fraction of one that replaces all three, even at the same finish level.
  • Labor market and region. A full house project uses nearly every trade (framing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, drywall, flooring, painting), so regional labor rates compound across every line item.
  • Permits and code compliance. Structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work are typically permitted and inspected separately, and older homes often need code upgrades that weren't part of the original plan.

The 3 Biggest "Invisible" Cost Drivers on a Full Gut

On a cosmetic or mid-range project, most of the budget goes toward things you can see. On a full gut, that flips — three systems you'll never look at again once the walls close up routinely account for the biggest jump between a mid-range number and a full-gut number.

1. Electrical: Whole-House Rewiring

  • Typical cost: $8,000 – $30,000, most commonly $10,000–$20,000 for an average single-family home

A full rewire replaces branch circuits, outlets, and switches, and frequently includes a panel upgrade to meet current code and handle modern electrical load. Older homes with knob-and-tube wiring or an undersized panel push toward the higher end, especially once the drywall patching that follows every rewire is factored in.

2. Plumbing: Whole-House Repipe

  • Typical cost: $6,000 – $20,000, most commonly $8,000–$15,000

Cost scales with the number of bathrooms and fixtures more than square footage alone. PEX has become the default material for most repipes because it costs less and installs faster than copper, though copper remains common in higher-end projects. A second story or a home on a slab typically adds labor time and cost.

3. HVAC: Full System Replacement

  • Typical cost: $5,000 – $20,000, most commonly $9,000–$15,000 for a standard furnace-and-AC split system

Reusing existing ductwork keeps costs toward the low end; adding new ductwork or upgrading to a heat pump or zoned system pushes toward $15,000–$20,000 or higher. One note if efficiency upgrades are part of the plan: the federal Section 25C energy-efficient home improvement tax credit was repealed effective January 1, 2026 under P.L. 119-21, so a high-efficiency system should be budgeted on its own merits rather than around that credit.

Combined, these three systems alone commonly run $20,000 to $70,000 on a full gut renovation — which is why two houses of identical size and finish level can land tens of thousands of dollars apart depending on what's actually behind the walls.

Budget vs. Mid-Range vs. High-End: What You Actually Get

Budget / cosmetic refresh projects leave the layout, structure, and systems untouched. Expect fresh paint throughout, new flooring, cabinet refacing instead of replacement, updated lighting and hardware, and a new toilet or vanity where needed. This tier has the fastest timeline of the three and typically requires no more than a basic electrical permit.

Mid-range renovations are the most common full-house scope nationally. They typically include a new kitchen, one or two fully renovated bathrooms, new flooring throughout, and updated fixtures and lighting, generally while keeping the existing floor plan intact or making one modest layout change. Plumbing and electrical work is usually targeted to the rooms being renovated rather than the whole house.

High-end / full gut renovations strip the home down to the studs and subfloor, then rebuild the electrical, plumbing, and often HVAC systems from scratch, along with any layout changes. This tier delivers the most complete transformation and is the only one that reliably addresses deferred maintenance hidden in the walls — but it also carries the lowest ROI of the three, a pattern covered in more detail below.

How to Finance a Full House Renovation

Because a full-house project is typically the largest single expense homeowners take on outside of buying the house itself, financing structure matters more here than on a single-room remodel. The common paths, in broad terms:

  • Cash-out refinance. Replaces your existing mortgage with a larger one and gives you the difference in cash, typically at a mortgage-level interest rate. Most useful when current mortgage rates are near or below your existing rate.
  • Home equity loan or HELOC. Borrows against the equity in your home as a second loan (fixed lump sum) or a revolving line of credit, leaving your first mortgage untouched. A HELOC's variable rate and draw structure suits a phased project well.
  • Renovation-specific loans. FHA 203(k) and Fannie Mae HomeStyle loans roll the purchase or refinance of a home and the renovation budget into a single mortgage, based on the home's projected post-renovation value rather than its current condition.
  • Personal savings or a personal loan. More common for the budget and mid-range tiers, where the total is small enough that mortgage-level financing isn't worth the closing costs and paperwork.

This is general information, not financial advice — a loan officer or financial advisor can weigh these against your specific rate, equity, and timeline.

Why Full House Renovations Have the Lowest ROI

Full house renovations return roughly 65% of cost at the budget tier, 55% at mid-range, and 45% at the high end. That's the lowest ROI of any project category SmartRenoCalc tracks, and consistently below single-room projects like kitchens or bathrooms. Two things drive that gap. First, appraisers and buyers compare a home to recent sales in the immediate neighborhood, not to the renovation budget behind it, so a house that becomes dramatically nicer than everything else on the block runs into a ceiling regardless of what was spent. Second, a large share of a full-house budget (the systems and structural work covered above) protects the sale rather than adding a premium on top of it, since buyers already expect a home's wiring, plumbing, and HVAC to work.

Ways to Control Full House Renovation Costs

  1. Get the scope right before you get quotes. The single biggest cost lever is which of the three tiers you're actually in — decide that first, in writing, before a contractor prices anything.
  2. Reuse systems that don't need replacing. Not every full-house project needs a full rewire or repipe. If an inspection shows the existing electrical and plumbing are sound, leaving them alone is the single largest savings available on a gut-level project.
  3. Keep the existing footprint. Structural and layout changes carry engineering, permitting, and framing costs on top of everything else — the same principle that controls cost on a single kitchen or bathroom applies at whole-house scale.
  4. Phase the project if cash flow requires it. Splitting a renovation into stages costs more in total than doing it all at once, but it avoids financing the entire project up front and allows more deliberate decisions on later-phase finishes. If the kitchen is part of your scope, our guide to kitchen remodeling savings covers specific ways to trim that line item on its own.
  5. Get at least three itemized bids. On a project this size, a wide spread on any single trade, whether electrical, plumbing, or framing, is worth a direct question before you sign anything.
  6. Budget a bigger contingency than usual. A single-room remodel typically budgets 10–15% for surprises; a full-house project, where every wall can hide something, should budget closer to 15–20%.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to renovate an entire house in 2026?

Most homeowners spend $30,000 to $400,000+, depending on home size and scope. A cosmetic refresh runs $15–$40 per square foot, a mid-range renovation runs $50–$100 per square foot, and a full gut renovation down to the studs runs $100–$200+ per square foot. On a typical 1,250–1,600 sq. ft. home, national data puts the average whole-house project around $19,500–$88,400 for standard scopes.

What's the difference between a renovation and a gut renovation?

A standard renovation updates surfaces and finishes such as paint, flooring, cabinets, and fixtures without touching the home's structure or systems. A gut renovation strips the interior down to the wall studs and subfloor, then rebuilds the electrical, plumbing, and often HVAC systems along with the layout. Gut renovations typically cost 3 to 5 times more per square foot than a standard cosmetic renovation.

Is it cheaper to renovate the whole house at once or room by room?

Renovating all at once is usually cheaper in total, since a contractor mobilizes crews, equipment, and permits once instead of repeatedly, and materials can be bought in bulk. Renovating room by room costs more overall but spreads the expense over a longer period and lets you keep living in more of the home while work is underway.

What part of a full house renovation costs the most?

Labor is the single largest expense, typically 40–50% of the total budget. Among specific rooms and systems, the kitchen is usually the biggest individual line item, followed by bathrooms and the combined cost of electrical, plumbing, and HVAC replacement on a full gut renovation.

Do full house renovations increase home value?

Yes, but they return a smaller share of their cost than most single-room remodels — typically 45% to 65% depending on quality tier, compared to 60–85% for kitchen and bathroom projects alone. That's because a large share of a full-house budget goes toward systems and structural work buyers already expect to function, rather than visible upgrades they'll pay a premium for.

How long does a full house renovation take?

A cosmetic refresh can wrap up in a few weeks to two months. A mid-range renovation covering several rooms typically takes 3 to 6 months. A full gut renovation with new systems, layout changes, and structural work commonly takes 4 to 12 months of construction, plus additional time for design and permitting before work begins.
